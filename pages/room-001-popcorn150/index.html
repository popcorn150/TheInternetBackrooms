A Brief History of Personal Computing

Early machines | The home computer | Desktop years

Last updated: November 17, 1999. Welcome to the Computer Archive. This page collects notes about machines, software, and the curious way they came to live on so many desks.

Early machines

Computers once filled rooms and demanded teams of operators. A program might arrive on punched cards. Results came back on paper, sometimes hours later. The idea of keeping one beside your telephone would have sounded extravagant.

Smaller components changed the scale of computing. Transistors replaced bulky vacuum tubes, integrated circuits packed many components together, and microprocessors placed a central processor onto a chip. Each step moved computers closer to ordinary people.

The home computer

By the late 1970s and early 1980s, hobbyists could buy computers for a desk at home. Many started with a blinking prompt. Owners typed commands, saved work to tapes or floppy disks, and learned quickly to keep a spare copy.

Home machines did more than calculate. People wrote letters, kept records, drew pictures, played games, and called distant bulletin boards over noisy telephone lines. A computer became a place to explore.

Graphical interfaces introduced windows, icons, menus, and a pointer. They gave files a visual home and made common tasks easier to discover. The desktop metaphor stuck, even when the computer itself no longer sat on a desk.

The desktop years

In the year , personal computers became familiar fixtures in schools and offices. Start-up sounds, crowded desktops, screen savers, and mysterious error messages were part of everyday computing. It was a busy time for operating systems and for the growing World Wide Web.

Browsers opened a new kind of window. A page could link to another page across the world. Some sites offered useful research; others had animated construction signs, visitor counters, and an impressive number of underlined blue links.

Software arrived on stacks of disks, then shiny discs. Installing it was sometimes an adventure of its own. A fast modem made the web feel almost instantaneous, provided nobody needed to use the phone.

The names and dates of old systems are easy to muddle together. A machine may have been manufactured in one year, sold widely in another, and remembered for a later update. Archives help keep the record straight.
